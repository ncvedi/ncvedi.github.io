Mining

Notes on mining and critical minerals: the supply chains behind batteries, electronics, and infrastructure, and the permitting and financing hurdles that stand between resources in the ground and usable supply.

  • Critical minerals. Lithium, copper, nickel, and rare earths, and why demand projections keep outrunning planned supply. Copper in particular sits at the intersection of the energy transition and data center buildout, and existing mines are aging and grading down even as electrification pushes demand higher; most forecasts show a structural deficit opening up later this decade unless a wave of new projects gets approved soon. Lithium has swung the other way, with prices collapsing from 2022 highs as Chinese supply and slower EV demand growth outran expectations, a reminder of how volatile these markets can be relative to their long-run demand story.
  • Permitting. Why new mines in most Western countries take a decade or more to reach production, and what's being proposed to fix it. In the U.S., a mine can spend more years in permitting and litigation than it does in construction and operation combined, which pushes capital toward jurisdictions with faster approval processes even when those carry more political or expropriation risk. Recent policy efforts (fast-track permitting for critical minerals, government equity stakes and offtake guarantees) reflect a growing recognition that the mine-to-market timeline is now a national security issue, not just a commercial one.
  • Processing and refining. The often-overlooked midstream of mining, where a small number of countries dominate capacity. China refines the large majority of the world's rare earths and a substantial share of lithium and cobalt, regardless of where the raw ore is mined, because it built out processing capacity while Western countries treated it as a low-margin, environmentally messy business. This concentration is now the more urgent bottleneck than mining itself, and export controls on rare earth processing have become a real geopolitical lever.
  • ESG and community impact. Balancing extraction with environmental and local community considerations. Tailings dam failures, water use conflicts, and Indigenous land rights disputes have killed or delayed major projects even where the mineral case was strong, and permitting reform proposals increasingly try to front-load community consent rather than litigate it after the fact. There's also a growing tension between the environmental case for mining more critical minerals (to enable electrification) and the local environmental cost of the mines themselves.
  • Capital markets. How financing cycles for junior miners shape which projects actually get built. Exploration is dominated by small-cap juniors who depend on equity markets to fund drilling, so a downturn in risk appetite can freeze project pipelines years before any supply shortage shows up in prices. Majors increasingly prefer to acquire proven deposits from juniors rather than explore themselves, which means M&A activity and juniors' access to capital are leading indicators for future mine supply.
