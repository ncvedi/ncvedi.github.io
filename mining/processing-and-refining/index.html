Processing and Refining

The often-overlooked midstream of mining, where a small number of countries—overwhelmingly China—dominate the capacity that turns raw ore into usable material.

  • China's processing dominance. China refines the large majority of the world's rare earths and a substantial share of the world's lithium and cobalt, regardless of where the raw ore is actually mined, because it built out processing capacity over decades while Western countries treated it as a low-margin, environmentally messy business. This means control over raw deposits doesn't necessarily translate into control over the finished material.
  • Export controls as leverage. This concentration is now a more urgent bottleneck than mining itself, and export controls on rare earth processing and magnet exports have become a real geopolitical lever, used and threatened amid trade tensions. A single country's processing dominance gives it outsized influence over industries as varied as electric vehicles, wind turbines, and defense systems.
  • Reshoring efforts. Western governments and companies have announced a wave of new processing facilities to reduce reliance on Chinese midstream capacity, but these projects face the same low-margin economics and environmental permitting challenges that caused Western capacity to shrink in the first place, in addition to years-long construction timelines. Subsidies and offtake guarantees are increasingly seen as necessary to make these projects viable against established Chinese competitors.
  • The economics of processing. Processing critical minerals into battery- or magnet-grade material is capital-intensive, chemically complex, and generates hazardous waste streams, which is why margins are often thinner than either mining or the downstream manufacturing that uses the finished material. This margin structure is part of why processing capacity concentrated in fewer hands than mining itself did.
  • Midstream as the real bottleneck. Because mining is comparatively more geographically diversified than processing, most forecasts of critical mineral shortages should really be read as forecasts of processing capacity shortages, since raw ore without processing capacity to refine it is of limited immediate use. This distinction shapes where governments are now directing industrial policy dollars.
