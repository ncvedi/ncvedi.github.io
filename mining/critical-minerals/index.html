Critical Minerals

Lithium, copper, nickel, and rare earths sit at the center of the energy transition and the electronics supply chain, and demand projections for them keep outrunning planned supply.

  • Copper's structural deficit. Copper sits at the intersection of the energy transition and the data center buildout, since both electrification and grid expansion are copper-intensive. Existing mines are aging and grading down even as electrification pushes demand higher, and most forecasts show a structural deficit opening up later this decade unless a wave of new projects gets approved soon—projects that typically take a decade or more to reach production.
  • Lithium's boom-bust cycle. Lithium prices collapsed from their 2022 highs as Chinese supply and slower-than-expected EV demand growth outran expectations, wiping out a huge amount of junior miner market value in the process. This volatility is a reminder that even minerals with a strong long-run demand story can see prices disconnect sharply from that story over a multi-year cycle, driven by short-term supply and demand mismatches.
  • Nickel and battery chemistry shifts. Nickel demand is increasingly tied to battery chemistry choices, and the industry has been shifting toward lower-nickel or nickel-free chemistries (like LFP) for cost and safety reasons in many EV and stationary storage applications. Indonesia's rapid buildout of nickel processing capacity has also reshaped the market, pushing prices down and squeezing higher-cost producers elsewhere.
  • Rare earths and magnet supply chains. Rare earths are needed in small quantities but are essential for permanent magnets used in EV motors, wind turbines, and defense applications, and China's dominance of both mining and processing gives it significant leverage over these supply chains. Efforts to build non-Chinese rare earth supply chains (Mountain Pass in the U.S., projects in Australia) have made progress but remain a small fraction of global capacity.
  • Substitution and recycling. Rising prices and supply security concerns have accelerated research into substitute materials and battery recycling, though recycling currently supplies only a small share of critical mineral demand since the stock of end-of-life batteries and electronics is still relatively young. Over the next decade, recycled material is expected to become a meaningfully larger share of supply as the first large wave of EV batteries reaches end of life.
