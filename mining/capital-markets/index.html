Capital Markets

How financing cycles for junior miners shape which projects actually get built, and why access to capital is often a better leading indicator of future supply than prices are.

  • Junior miners and equity dependence. Exploration is dominated by small-cap junior mining companies who depend almost entirely on equity markets to fund drilling programs, since they typically have no revenue to borrow against. A downturn in risk appetite among retail and specialist mining investors can freeze project pipelines years before any resulting supply shortage shows up in commodity prices.
  • Majors acquiring rather than exploring. Major mining companies increasingly prefer to acquire proven deposits from juniors through M&A rather than run their own exploration programs, since exploration success rates are low and majors' capital is often better deployed de-risking and developing known deposits. This means M&A activity and juniors' access to capital are leading indicators for future mine supply, arguably more informative than exploration spending alone.
  • Streaming and royalty financing. Streaming and royalty companies provide upfront capital to miners in exchange for a share of future production or revenue, offering a financing route that doesn't dilute equity or add conventional debt. This has become a significant financing channel, particularly for mid-size projects that are too large for junior-style equity raises but not attractive enough for traditional project debt.
  • Cyclicality and capital flight. Mining capital markets are famously cyclical: bull markets attract a flood of speculative capital into exploration-stage juniors, while bear markets can shut most of them out of financing entirely, regardless of the quality of their underlying projects. This boom-bust financing pattern is a structural reason why mine supply tends to lag price signals by many years.
  • Sovereign and strategic capital. Government-backed financing, sovereign wealth fund investment, and state-linked offtake agreements (particularly from China, but increasingly from Western governments targeting critical minerals) have become a larger share of project financing, partly displacing the traditional reliance on public equity markets for larger, strategically important projects.
