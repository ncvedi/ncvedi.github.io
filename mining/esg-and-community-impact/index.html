ESG and Community Impact

Balancing extraction with environmental and local community considerations, and the growing tension between mining more minerals to enable the energy transition and the local costs of doing so.

  • Tailings dam failures. Catastrophic tailings dam failures, like Brazil's Brumadinho disaster, have killed hundreds of people and pushed the industry and regulators toward stricter tailings management standards, but the sheer number of aging tailings facilities worldwide means the risk hasn't gone away. These failures have also made tailings management a bigger factor in project financing and insurance costs.
  • Water use conflicts. Mining, particularly for lithium brine extraction and copper processing, is often water-intensive in regions that are already water-stressed, creating direct conflicts with agricultural and local community water use. These conflicts have delayed or blocked major projects even where the underlying mineral economics were strong, making water rights a first-order permitting issue rather than a secondary environmental concern.
  • Indigenous land rights. Disputes over Indigenous land rights and free, prior, and informed consent have delayed or killed major projects across Australia, Canada, and Latin America, and courts have increasingly sided with Indigenous communities in cases where consultation was seen as inadequate. This has pushed some companies and governments toward front-loading community consent processes earlier in project development rather than litigating disputes after the fact.
  • Consent-based permitting reform. Reform proposals increasingly try to build community consent into the early stages of permitting rather than treating it as a box to check near the end, on the theory that early, genuine engagement reduces the litigation risk that has made timelines so unpredictable. Whether these reforms meaningfully speed up approvals or simply move the friction earlier in the process is still being tested.
  • The transition's internal tension. There's a growing tension between the environmental case for mining more critical minerals—to enable electrification and reduce fossil fuel use—and the local environmental and social cost of the mines themselves. This tension doesn't have an easy resolution and increasingly shapes how governments, investors, and environmental groups position themselves on individual projects rather than mining as a category.
