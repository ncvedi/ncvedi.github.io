Permitting

Why new mines in most Western countries take a decade or more to reach production, and what's being proposed to fix it.

  • Multi-year, multi-agency timelines. In the U.S., a mine can spend more years in permitting and litigation than it does in construction and operation combined, requiring approvals from multiple federal and state agencies that don't always coordinate on timelines. This pushes capital toward jurisdictions with faster approval processes even when those carry more political or expropriation risk.
  • NEPA and litigation risk. Environmental review under NEPA and similar frameworks creates multiple points where opponents can sue to delay or block a project, and even a successful defense of a permit can take years and tie up capital with no guarantee of ever reaching production. This legal uncertainty is often cited by miners as a bigger deterrent to investment than the underlying environmental standards themselves.
  • Fast-track policy proposals. Recent policy efforts—fast-track permitting designations for critical minerals projects, government equity stakes, and offtake guarantees—reflect a growing recognition that the mine-to-market timeline is now treated as a national security issue, not just a commercial one. These programs aim to compress timelines and de-risk financing simultaneously, though most are still early and untested at scale.
  • Jurisdictional risk tradeoffs. Miners increasingly weigh permitting speed against political and expropriation risk when choosing where to develop projects; a jurisdiction that approves projects quickly may also be more prone to later changing royalty terms, nationalizing assets, or reversing approvals with a change in government. This tradeoff is a recurring theme in capital allocation decisions across Africa, Latin America, and Southeast Asia.
  • Government equity and offtake support. Some governments have moved beyond streamlined permitting to actively co-invest in projects or guarantee offtake, effectively underwriting the demand side of a mine's economics to attract private capital faster. This marks a shift from purely regulatory reform toward direct industrial policy for strategic minerals.
