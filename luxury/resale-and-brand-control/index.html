Resale and Brand Control

How the secondhand luxury market grew from an informal consignment business into a large, semi-institutional one, and how brands went from resisting it to trying to own it.

  • Growth of the secondary market. Platforms like The RealReal, Vestiaire Collective, and StockX turned what used to be a scattered, informal consignment-shop business into a large, venture-backed market, giving buyers a much more accessible entry point to the category than first-hand retail pricing allows.
  • Brand resistance. Many luxury houses historically viewed resale as diluting to brand exclusivity and pricing power, with some refusing to authenticate or service items bought secondhand. The underlying worry was that a visible discount in the resale market would undermine confidence in the primary retail price.
  • Brands embracing certified resale. That stance has shifted meaningfully: several maisons, including brands under LVMH and Richemont's acquisition of the pre-owned watch platform Watchfinder, now run or partner on official certified pre-owned programs, aiming to capture resale margin themselves and control quality and counterfeit risk rather than cede the market to third parties entirely.
  • Sustainability framing. Resale is also marketed heavily as a sustainability play, extending a garment's or accessory's usable lifecycle and reducing the environmental footprint of new production, which helps brands participate in secondary markets without it reading as an admission that first-hand demand has softened.
  • Watches as the leading edge. Resale dynamics are most visible and price-transparent in the watch market, where indices tracking secondary prices for models like the Rolex Daytona or Patek Philippe Nautilus are watched closely across the industry as a leading indicator of category health, a dynamic covered in more detail in the notes on watches and hard luxury.
