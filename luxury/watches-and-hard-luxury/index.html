Watches and Hard Luxury

How deliberate scarcity built one of luxury's most extreme gray markets, and what its recent boom-and-correction cycle says about speculative demand in the category more broadly.

  • Waitlist economics. Brands like Rolex and Patek Philippe deliberately under-supply retail demand for their most desirable models, creating years-long official waitlists at authorized dealers. That gap pushes buyers who want immediate delivery toward the gray market, where dealers charge substantial premiums over retail price.
  • The 2021–2022 speculative peak. Pandemic stimulus, low interest rates, and easy money drove gray market and secondary watch prices to extreme premiums, with some steel sports models trading for multiples of their official retail price. That run-up attracted purely speculative buyers with no real interest in the watches themselves, rather than the collectors the market had historically served.
  • The correction. Secondary watch prices fell sharply and steadily through 2022–2024 as interest rates rose and speculative buyers exited the market. Indices tracking models like the Nautilus and Daytona show premiums compressing significantly from their peak, though the most sought-after references have generally continued to trade above retail even after the correction.
  • Brand response. Rather than raising official retail prices to capture the premium themselves — which risks alienating loyal retail clients and permanently repricing the brand upward — watchmakers have mostly held retail pricing steady while modestly expanding production and being more deliberate about which clients and purchase histories get access to the hardest-to-find references.
  • Vertical integration into pre-owned. Richemont's acquisition of the pre-owned watch platform Watchfinder, along with similar moves by other groups, reflects brands wanting a cut of the resale economics rather than ceding that market to third parties, and gives them a way to monitor and influence gray market flow more directly.
