Direct-to-Consumer and Retail

Why luxury brands have steadily pulled control of distribution back from wholesale partners, and how their physical stores function as much as marketing as they do as points of sale.

  • Wholesale to DTC shift. Luxury brands have steadily reduced their reliance on department stores and multi-brand wholesale in favor of directly operated boutiques, which gives them full control over pricing, presentation, and the customer data that wholesale partners previously captured for themselves.
  • Flagship stores as marketing, not just retail. Flagship locations on streets like Fifth Avenue, Bond Street, and Avenue Montaigne are often unprofitable as standalone retail units once real estate costs are factored in, but brands justify them anyway as brand marketing and reputational investments rather than as businesses that need to pencil out on their own.
  • Real estate as strategic asset. LVMH and other groups have increasingly bought the real estate underlying key flagship locations outright rather than leasing it, both to lock in prime locations against competitors bidding for the same limited real estate and because the properties themselves appreciate as assets independent of the retail business inside them.
  • Travel retail dependence and disruption. Before the shift toward DTC, airport and duty-free travel retail, including LVMH-owned DFS, was a major channel for reaching Chinese outbound travelers in particular. The pandemic-era collapse in international travel and its slow, uneven recovery exposed how dependent that channel had become on a fairly narrow set of travel corridors.
  • E-commerce's limited penetration. Luxury goods have lagged most other retail categories in online sales share, since high-value purchases benefit from in-person service, authentication comfort, and experiential selling that brands have been reluctant to fully replicate online, though this is slowly changing for smaller accessory and beauty categories that carry less purchase friction.
