Conglomerates and Brand Portfolios

How three companies came to control most of the world's storied luxury maisons, and the tension between scaling revenue and preserving the exclusivity that justifies premium pricing.

  • The big three. LVMH spans roughly 75 maisons across fashion, wine and spirits, watches and jewelry, beauty, and retail including Sephora and DFS. Kering is far more concentrated, historically deriving over half of group profit from Gucci alone, while Richemont leans heavily toward jewelry and watches through brands like Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels. That difference in portfolio construction means each group carries a very different risk profile.
  • Why conglomeration works. Owning dozens of brands under one roof gives shared negotiating power for flagship real estate, shared manufacturing capacity in leather goods and other ateliers, shared marketing and celebrity relationships, and a deeper executive talent pipeline than any standalone maison could afford to build alone.
  • Single-brand concentration risk. Kering's heavy reliance on Gucci meant that brand's creative and commercial stumble starting in 2023 hit group results far harder than any single brand's softness affected LVMH, which is diversified across dozens of maisons. The episode pushed Kering into a new creative direction and a CEO change, and made the risk of over-concentration in one brand a live case study across the industry.
  • Heritage and scarcity as the moat. The entire luxury model depends on maintaining an aura of exclusivity even while scaling revenue into the tens of billions of dollars, which creates constant tension between quarterly growth targets — more stores, more product, more markets — and the mystique that justifies premium pricing in the first place. LVMH's Bernard Arnault has spoken publicly about deliberately limiting availability of certain products rather than chasing every unit of demand.
  • M&A as growth strategy. Conglomerates grow partly by acquiring independent or family-owned maisons, as LVMH did with Tiffany & Co. Acquisition prices for the remaining desirable independent luxury houses of real scale have risen as the pool of targets shrinks, which has pushed some groups to focus more on building new brands in-house rather than competing to buy the few left.
