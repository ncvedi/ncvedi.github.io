Luxury

Notes on the global luxury industry: how a handful of conglomerates built durable pricing power around scarcity and heritage, and where that model is being tested.

  • Conglomerates and brand portfolios. LVMH, Kering, and Richemont between them control dozens of storied maisons, using shared scale in real estate, marketing, and supply chains while trying to preserve the individual exclusivity that justifies each brand's premium pricing. Kering's heavy reliance on a single brand, Gucci, exposed it to a much sharper earnings hit during that brand's recent slump than LVMH felt from any one label's softness.
  • China and global demand. Chinese consumers, both domestic and outbound, became the single largest driver of luxury industry growth through the 2010s, and their sharp pullback since 2023 amid a property crisis and weaker consumer confidence has been the biggest swing factor in the sector's revenue trajectory since. The slowdown has hit aspirational, entry-level luxury buyers hardest, while demand at the very top of the market has stayed comparatively resilient.
  • Resale and brand control. A once-informal secondhand market has become a large, VC-backed business through platforms like The RealReal and Vestiaire Collective, which brands initially resisted as diluting to exclusivity but have increasingly moved to embrace and control directly, including through their own certified pre-owned programs.
  • Watches and hard luxury. Rolex and Patek Philippe deliberately under-supply their most desirable models, creating years-long waitlists and a gray market where buyers pay substantial premiums for immediate delivery. That gray market saw an extreme speculative run-up in 2021–2022 followed by a steady, multi-year correction as rates rose and speculative buyers exited.
  • Direct-to-consumer and retail. Brands have steadily shifted away from wholesale and duty-free travel retail toward directly operated boutiques, with flagship stores on prime real estate functioning as marketing investments as much as points of sale — a shift significant enough that some groups now buy the underlying real estate outright rather than lease it.
