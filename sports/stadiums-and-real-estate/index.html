Stadiums and Real Estate

How stadium financing shifted from taxpayers to owners, and why the venues themselves increasingly double as real estate developments.

  • Declining appetite for public financing. Stadium subsidies funded largely by taxpayers were the norm for decades, but public and political resistance has grown as studies have consistently found minimal broad economic benefit to host cities. That's pushed more of the cost onto team owners even as total project costs have risen into the billions.
  • Stadiums as real estate anchors. Newer stadium projects, like SoFi Stadium and its surrounding Hollywood Park development or the Texas Rangers' Globe Life Field district, are increasingly built as the centerpiece of a larger mixed-use development with retail, housing, and offices. That lets owners capture non-gameday revenue and real estate appreciation rather than relying on ticket and concession income alone.
  • Self-financing and private capital. As public subsidies shrink, owners are financing more of stadium costs themselves or through private capital, and hybrid structures combining the NFL's stadium loan program with municipal and private financing have become more common ways to bridge the gap.
  • Naming rights and non-gameday revenue. Corporate naming rights deals, like those for SoFi Stadium, Allegiant Stadium, and Crypto.com Arena, now run into the hundreds of millions of dollars over the life of a deal, and venues increasingly host concerts, esports, and other non-sports events to keep generating revenue on the roughly 340 nights a year there isn't a home game.
  • Relocation as leverage. The threat of relocating to another city remains one of the most effective tools owners have to extract concessions or financing from local governments, even when an actual move is unlikely, because the credible alternative of losing a franchise carries outsized political cost for local officials.
