Franchise Valuations

Why sports franchise prices keep climbing well ahead of what the teams actually earn, and what that says about who's buying and why.

  • Scarcity value. The number of franchises in the NFL, NBA, MLB, NHL, and the top European soccer leagues is essentially fixed, with expansion rare and deliberate, so owning one is closer to owning a scarce, non-fungible trophy asset than a normal cash-flow-generating business. Valuations reflect that scarcity far more than they reflect current profitability.
  • Valuations decoupling from operating income. Recent sale prices — the Washington Commanders at roughly 6.05 billion dollars in 2023, the Denver Broncos at roughly 4.65 billion dollars in 2022 — implied revenue multiples far above what a typical private business would command. Buyers justify those prices less on near-term cash flow and more on expected media rights growth, long-run appreciation, and the prestige and access that come with ownership itself.
  • Debt and financing rule changes. Leagues have historically capped how much debt a buyer can use to finance a purchase. As prices have risen, leagues have had to loosen those debt limits and, increasingly, allow institutional and private equity minority stakes, simply because the pool of individuals who can write a multi-billion-dollar check in cash has narrowed even among billionaires.
  • Illiquidity and infrequent sales. Because there are so few franchises and owners tend to hold for decades, often across generations, comparable sales are rare. Each transaction becomes a major price-setting event that resets valuation expectations for the rest of the league, sometimes overnight.
  • Soccer's different model. European soccer clubs, especially the publicly traded ones like Manchester United and Juventus, price partly on sporting and investment logic and are exposed to relegation risk that can destroy a large share of a club's value in a single season — a risk that closed-league U.S. franchises, which can never be relegated, don't face at all.
