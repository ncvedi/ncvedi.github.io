Travel

Notes from places I've traveled.

  • Singapore. A city-state that runs like a piece of engineered infrastructure: efficient transit, relentless land reclamation, and a skyline that feels planned down to the last sightline. The food courts (hawker centers) are the real cultural core, serving Chinese, Malay, and Indian food side by side for a few dollars a plate, a better window into Singapore's multiethnic makeup than any museum. What stands out most is how deliberate everything feels, from the greenery woven into every building to the strict rules that keep the place spotless and orderly.
  • France. Paris rewards walking more than any itinerary: the museums (the Louvre, the Musée d'Orsay) are essential, but the city's real pleasure is in the cafés, boulangeries, and the Seine at dusk. Outside the capital, the pace slows considerably — the countryside, the vineyards, and the smaller towns move on their own schedule and are worth the trip specifically to get away from the tourist density of Paris. The French relationship to food and mealtime, unhurried and taken seriously, is the thing most worth absorbing.
  • The UK. London layers two thousand years of history into a single walk — Roman walls, medieval churches, Georgian squares, Victorian rail stations — and the museums (the British Museum, the National Gallery) are free, which makes repeat visits easy. Beyond London, the countryside and smaller cities (the Cotswolds, Edinburgh, York) show a different, slower England and Scotland, one built around pubs, footpaths, and a much older sense of place. The weather is as unreliable as advertised, but it rarely gets in the way.
  • Portugal. Lisbon's hills and pastel tilework give it a texture that's distinct from anywhere else in Europe, and it remains noticeably cheaper than its Western European peers while offering comparable food and infrastructure. Porto, to the north, trades Lisbon's sprawl for a compact, riverside old town built around the port wine trade, and the Douro Valley just inland is one of the more underrated wine regions in Europe. The Portuguese seem less interested than their neighbors in performing their history for tourists, which makes the place feel more lived-in.
  • Japan. Tokyo and Kyoto together capture the country's central contrast: hyper-modern, efficient urbanism against meticulously preserved temples, shrines, and gardens, often within the same city block. The trains run on time to the minute, the food (from convenience-store onigiri to kaiseki) is reliably excellent at every price point, and the overall level of public courtesy and cleanliness is hard to overstate until you've experienced it. It's a country where the old and new don't feel in tension so much as deliberately layered on top of each other.
  • Vietnam. Hanoi's Old Quarter and Ho Chi Minh City's traffic-choked streets are both organized chaos in a way that takes a day or two to adjust to, but the street food scene — pho, banh mi, egg coffee — is worth the adjustment on its own. Ha Long Bay and the countryside around Hoi An offer a much quieter counterpoint to the cities, with limestone karsts and rice paddies that feel like a different country entirely. The scars of the twentieth century are still visible and openly discussed, which gives even a short trip a heavier historical weight than most Southeast Asian itineraries.
