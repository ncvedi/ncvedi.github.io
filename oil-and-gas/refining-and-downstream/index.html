Refining and Downstream

How refiners turn crude into usable fuels, and why a business built on thin margins and heavy fixed costs has become more consolidated and more volatile at the same time.

  • No new large refineries. No new large-scale U.S. refinery has been built from scratch in decades, largely because of permitting difficulty, environmental opposition, and uncertain long-term demand for refined products in a decarbonizing world. Nearly all capacity growth has instead come from debottlenecking and incremental expansions of existing plants.
  • Closures outpacing additions. Refining capacity has been closing faster than it's being added across the U.S. and Europe, as older, less complex refineries become uneconomic to upgrade to meet fuel-quality and emissions standards. This shrinking capacity base tightens crack spreads (the margin between crude input costs and refined product prices) whenever demand is strong or unplanned outages take capacity offline.
  • Crack spreads and utilization. Refining margins are driven less by the level of crude prices than by the spread between crude and product prices, which widens when refinery utilization is high and narrows when there's spare capacity. Utilization rates above roughly 90–95% tend to signal a tight market where any unplanned outage can send product prices sharply higher.
  • Shifting product demand. Gasoline demand growth has flattened in mature markets as vehicle fuel efficiency improves and EV adoption grows, while diesel and jet fuel demand have proven more resilient, tied to freight, aviation, and industrial activity. Refiners with more flexible configurations can shift their product mix toward whichever fuel is in higher demand, which is a growing source of competitive advantage.
  • Petrochemical integration. Many refiners have integrated forward into petrochemicals—producing plastics feedstocks like ethylene and propylene—as a hedge against flattening transportation fuel demand, since petrochemical demand is tied more to plastics and manufacturing growth than to vehicle miles traveled. This integration is more common and more advanced in Asia and the Middle East than in the U.S.
