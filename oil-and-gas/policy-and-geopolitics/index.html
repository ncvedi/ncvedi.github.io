Policy and Geopolitics

How OPEC+ decisions, sanctions regimes, and the politics of energy security shape investment and prices as much as pure supply-and-demand fundamentals do.

  • OPEC+ dynamics. OPEC+ has been unwinding voluntary production cuts to defend market share against non-OPEC supply growth from the U.S., Guyana, and Brazil, a strategic shift after several years of holding back production to support prices. This unwind caps upside in oil prices even amid geopolitical risk premiums, since spare capacity within the group can be brought online relatively quickly if prices spike.
  • Sanctions regimes. Sanctions on Russia, Iran, and Venezuela have created a parallel "shadow fleet" of tankers and buyers willing to move sanctioned barrels at a discount, which has kept most sanctioned oil flowing to market (mainly to China and India) rather than removing it entirely. This means sanctions tend to redirect trade flows and compress discounts rather than durably remove supply, which is a recurring lesson across multiple sanctions episodes.
  • Energy security versus decarbonization. Energy security has become as important a driver of policy as decarbonization, especially after 2022 exposed how exposed Europe was to a single supplier. This has pushed Europe toward diversified LNG supply and renewed domestic production debates, and pushed the U.S. toward permitting reform discussions that cut across party lines, since both climate-motivated transmission projects and traditional energy infrastructure face the same bottlenecks.
  • Strategic reserves. Government-held strategic petroleum reserves are a key tool for managing short-term price shocks, and their drawdown and refill cycles (as seen in the U.S. after 2022) can meaningfully affect near-term prices and are watched closely by traders as a signal of policy intent. Reserve levels relative to historical norms are also a factor in how much cushion exists against a genuine supply disruption.
  • Permitting reform politics. Efforts to speed up permitting for energy infrastructure—pipelines, transmission, LNG terminals—have found unusual bipartisan interest, since slow permitting frustrates both traditional energy development and the clean energy buildout alike. Legislative permitting reform has repeatedly stalled despite this shared interest, reflecting how differently the two coalitions prioritize which specific projects should benefit.
