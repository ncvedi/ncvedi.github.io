LNG

How liquefied natural gas turned regional gas markets into something closer to a global commodity market, and what the current wave of U.S. export capacity means for prices.

  • The U.S. export buildout. The U.S. has become the world's largest LNG exporter, and a wave of new liquefaction capacity—Plaquemines, Corpus Christi Stage III, Golden Pass, and others—is set to come online through the back half of the decade. This buildout should ease the tightness in global gas markets that followed Russia's cut-off of pipeline gas to Europe in 2022.
  • Offtake contracts and destination flexibility. Long-term offtake contracts increasingly go to buyers in Asia rather than Europe, reflecting where demand growth is actually happening as countries like India, China, and emerging Southeast Asian markets build out gas-fired power and industrial demand. Many U.S. contracts are also destination-flexible, letting buyers redirect cargoes to whichever market offers the best price, which is part of why U.S. LNG has become a global price-setting mechanism.
  • Feed gas and liquefaction economics. LNG project economics hinge on cheap, abundant feed gas (which the U.S. Gulf Coast has thanks to shale production) combined with the capital cost of liquefaction trains, which run into the billions of dollars and take years to construct. Project developers typically need a critical mass of long-term offtake agreements signed before reaching a final investment decision, which is why the pace of new project announcements is a leading indicator of supply years out.
  • Rewiring global gas markets. Before the LNG boom, natural gas markets were largely regional, connected by pipelines and priced very differently in the U.S., Europe, and Asia; LNG has increasingly arbitraged those markets together, though shipping costs and liquefaction/regasification capacity still limit full convergence. This has made European and Asian gas prices far more sensitive to U.S. weather, production, and export facility outages than they were a decade ago.
  • Shipping and regasification. The LNG value chain also depends on a fleet of specialized tankers and regasification terminals at the receiving end, both of which can become bottlenecks; Europe's rapid buildout of floating storage and regasification units (FSRUs) after 2022 was a notable example of how quickly import infrastructure can be added compared with liquefaction capacity, which takes years to build.
