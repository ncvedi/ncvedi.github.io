Midstream Infrastructure

The pipelines, storage, and processing assets that connect production to markets, and why building new capacity in the U.S. has become so much harder than operating existing capacity.

  • Permitting bottlenecks. NEPA environmental reviews, state-level litigation, and local opposition have made greenfield interstate pipeline construction in the U.S. slow, expensive, and legally uncertain, with projects like the Mountain Valley Pipeline taking years longer and costing multiples of their original budget. This risk premium is now priced into how midstream companies plan new capacity.
  • Debottlenecking over greenfield. Because new pipeline construction is so contested, most recent midstream investment has gone into debottlenecking and expanding existing corridors—adding pump stations, looping segments, or upgrading compression—rather than building brand-new rights of way. This is generally cheaper and faster to permit, but it has finite capacity to absorb growing production.
  • Takeaway capacity and basin discounts. When production in a basin outgrows the pipeline capacity to move it out, local prices can fall sharply relative to benchmark prices; this has happened repeatedly with Permian natural gas and, historically, Bakken crude. Associated gas produced alongside oil has occasionally traded at negative prices in the Permian when takeaway capacity was full and operators had to flare or pay to dispose of it rather than shut in the oil well.
  • Storage economics. Crude and product storage capacity matters most during periods of contango (future prices above spot), when traders can profit by storing barrels now and selling forward contracts; storage utilization is a closely watched signal of oversupply. Underground natural gas storage plays a similar seasonal role, filling in summer to meet winter heating demand.
  • Regulatory framework. Interstate pipelines fall under FERC's jurisdiction, while intrastate lines and gathering systems are largely regulated at the state level, creating a patchwork that midstream companies have to navigate project by project. Rate-of-return regulation on regulated pipelines also shapes how these assets are financed, typically favoring stable, bond-like capital structures over the more cyclical financing used upstream.
