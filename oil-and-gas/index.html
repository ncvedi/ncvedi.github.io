Oil & Gas

Notes on upstream, midstream, and downstream oil & gas: exploration economics, drilling activity, capital discipline, and how the industry is adapting to a more volatile pricing environment.

  • Upstream economics. Break-even costs, decline curves, and how shale operators have shifted from growth-at-all-costs to free cash flow discipline. Public E&Ps are largely returning capital to shareholders (buybacks, dividends) rather than chasing production growth, which caps how fast U.S. supply can respond to price spikes. Tier-1 acreage in the Permian is being drilled down, pushing operators toward lower-quality inventory and consolidation (Exxon-Pioneer, Chevron-Hess, Diamondback-Endeavor) as a way to refill drilling inventory rather than explore for new plays.
  • Midstream infrastructure. Pipelines, storage, and the permitting bottlenecks that increasingly determine which projects get built. NEPA reviews, state-level litigation, and local opposition have made greenfield pipeline construction in the U.S. slow and expensive, which is part of why so much recent midstream investment has gone into debottlenecking and expanding existing corridors rather than building new ones. Takeaway capacity constraints (Permian gas, Bakken oil historically) have repeatedly forced basin-level price discounts and even negative pricing for associated gas.
  • Refining and downstream. Margins, utilization, and the slow reshaping of refining capacity as demand growth flattens in mature markets. No new large-scale U.S. refinery has been built from scratch in decades; capacity growth has come from debottlenecking existing plants. Refining capacity has been closing faster in the U.S. and Europe than it's being added, tightening crack spreads during periods of high demand and making the sector more cyclical and headline-sensitive to unplanned outages.
  • LNG. The buildout of export capacity and how it's rewiring global gas markets. The U.S. has become the world's largest LNG exporter, and a wave of new liquefaction capacity (Plaquemines, Corpus Christi Stage III, Golden Pass, and others) is set to come online through the back half of the decade, which should ease the tightness that followed Russia's cut-off of pipeline gas to Europe. Long-term offtake contracts increasingly go to buyers in Asia rather than Europe, reflecting where demand growth is actually happening.
  • Policy and geopolitics. OPEC+ dynamics, sanctions, and how energy security concerns are reshaping investment decisions. OPEC+ has been unwinding voluntary production cuts to defend market share against non-OPEC supply growth (U.S., Guyana, Brazil), which caps upside in oil prices even amid geopolitical risk premiums from the Middle East, Russia sanctions, and shipping disruptions in the Red Sea. Energy security has become as important a driver of policy as decarbonization, pushing Europe toward diversified LNG supply and the U.S. toward permitting reform debates that cut across party lines.
