Art

Notes on the art market: how value gets established for an inherently illiquid, unregulated asset, and how the industry's economics differ from almost anything else being bought and sold at this scale.

  • Auction market. How Christie's and Sotheby's actually make money, and why the visible hammer price is only part of the transaction. Both houses went private in the past two decades, which lets them offer aggressive guarantees to secure marquee consignments, and a growing share of high-end business now happens through quiet private sales rather than the public spectacle of an evening auction. Contemporary art auction sales have declined significantly since their 2022 peak as higher rates pulled speculative buyers out of the middle of the market, even as trophy lots from blue-chip names keep fetching strong prices.
  • Art as an asset class. Why decades of attempts to financialize art — funds, indices, fractional ownership — have struggled against the asset's basic illiquidity and price opacity. Platforms like Masterworks have made fractional ownership accessible, but their economics rely more on management and sales fees than on realized appreciation, and art-backed lending has quietly become a bigger business than art funds ever were, letting collectors raise cash without selling. The market's returns track the wealth of the ultra-rich far more closely than they track any broad economic indicator.
  • Galleries and the primary market. The gallery system that sets prices for living artists before their work ever reaches auction. A handful of mega-galleries (Gagosian, Pace, David Zwirner, Hauser & Wirth) now operate like global retail chains, squeezing mid-size galleries out of art fairs and artist representation, while hot artists' galleries often sell to curated buyer lists specifically to prevent the kind of flipping that would create visible price volatility.
  • Authentication and provenance. How the market decides a work is genuine, and why that system is quietly breaking down. Litigation risk has driven several major artists' foundations, including Warhol's and Basquiat's, to disband their authentication boards entirely, even after winning the underlying lawsuits, leaving today's market without an authoritative living voice on many artists' bodies of work.
  • Ultra-contemporary and speculation. The boom and bust in work by young, recently-emerged artists, which became the market's most speculative corner during the pandemic-era wealth surge. Ultra-contemporary prices saw some of the sharpest drawdowns anywhere in the art market as speculative buyers exited in 2023 and 2024, even as a separate and more durable shift — a much larger market for women artists and artists of color — has continued regardless.
