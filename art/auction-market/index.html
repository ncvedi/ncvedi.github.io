Auction Market

How the big auction houses actually make money, and why the visible hammer price at a headline sale is only part of the transaction.

  • The Christie's/Sotheby's duopoly. Christie's and Sotheby's have dominated the high end of the market for over two centuries, with Phillips a distant third focused mostly on postwar and contemporary work. Both major houses are now privately owned — Sotheby's by Patrick Drahi since 2019, Christie's by the Pinault family — which frees them from quarterly public reporting pressure and lets them offer far more aggressive financial terms to win consignments than a public company comfortable disclosing risk to shareholders would.
  • Buyer's premium and seller's terms. Buyers pay a premium on top of the hammer price, commonly in the 20–27% range and structured on a tiered scale that decreases as a percentage at higher price bands, which is a major and growing source of house revenue independent of what the seller receives. Sellers of trophy lots, by contrast, increasingly pay reduced or even zero commission, because houses compete hard on economics to win the consignment of works likely to generate the publicity and buyer's premium of a marquee sale.
  • Guarantees and irrevocable bids. To secure a major consignment, a house will often guarantee the seller a minimum price regardless of what happens at auction, then lay off some of that risk to a third-party guarantor who commits an "irrevocable bid" in exchange for a fee and a share of any upside. This financializes what looks like spontaneous live bidding into something closer to a pre-negotiated transaction with a public unveiling, and it's one reason a widely reported record price doesn't always reflect genuine open-market demand.
  • Evening sales, day sales, and private sales. Evening sales showcase the marquee lots under time pressure and theater designed to drive prices up; day sales handle higher-volume, lower-value material with far less ceremony. A growing share of the houses' actual business now happens through private sales negotiated quietly off the auction floor, which avoids the reputational damage of a trophy lot going unsold ("bought in") in public, an outcome that can depress an artist's market for years afterward.
  • The post-2022 cooldown. Postwar and contemporary auction sales boomed in 2021–2022 on the back of low rates and a wave of new, often crypto-adjacent wealth entering the market, then declined significantly as rates rose and that speculative buying pool retreated. The pullback has hit the middle of the market hardest; trophy lots from blue-chip, historically established artists have continued to find buyers even as overall sale volumes and estimates have come down.
