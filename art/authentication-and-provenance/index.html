Authentication and Provenance

How the market decides a work is genuine, and why that system has been quietly breaking down over the past two decades.

  • Catalogues raisonnés. The traditional, definitive scholarly record of an artist's complete body of work, compiled by scholars or a foundation and used as the reference point for authentication. The years after an artist's death, before such a catalogue can be compiled and a governing authority established, leave the market for that artist's work especially vulnerable to disputed or fraudulent pieces.
  • Litigation risk shutting down authentication boards. Several major artists' foundations, including the Andy Warhol Foundation and the Jean-Michel Basquiat estate's authentication efforts, have disbanded their authentication committees after being sued by owners of works the board declined to authenticate. Even when the foundations won the underlying lawsuits, the legal costs ran into the millions, and the practical result is that today many of the most valuable and heavily forged artists have no authoritative living body willing to rule on authenticity at all.
  • Forgery scandals. The 2011 collapse of New York's Knoedler Gallery, one of the country's oldest, after it was found to have sold tens of millions of dollars in forged Abstract Expressionist works with fabricated provenance histories, remains the market's most cited cautionary tale. It shifted serious buyers' attention toward documented chain-of-custody as much as toward traditional connoisseurship-based authentication.
  • Looted and Nazi-era provenance. Restitution claims continue to surface for works with unexplained gaps in ownership history during the WWII era, and major museums and auction houses now maintain dedicated provenance research departments to screen for this risk. A title dispute of this kind can freeze or kill a sale entirely even when there's no question about the work's authenticity.
  • Digital provenance and technology. Blockchain-based certificates of authenticity and NFT-linked "digital twins" have been pitched repeatedly as a fix for provenance gaps, but adoption has stayed limited mostly to newly created work. The approach doesn't solve the harder underlying problem of documenting decades or centuries of pre-digital ownership history for existing works, which is where most authentication disputes actually arise.
