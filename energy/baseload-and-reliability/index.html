Baseload and Reliability

How grid operators are managing the trade-off between decarbonization and reliability as the generation mix shifts faster than replacement capacity can be built.

  • Retirements outpacing replacements. Coal and some gas plants are retiring faster than firm, dispatchable replacements are coming online, since new gas, nuclear, and long-duration storage capacity all face their own construction and interconnection delays. This mismatch is the core mechanical reason reliability concerns have grown even as overall generation capacity, on paper, keeps increasing.
  • Tightening reserve margins. Reserve margins—the buffer of extra capacity above expected peak demand—have tightened in several U.S. grid regions, and grid operators have issued increasingly frequent reliability warnings, especially heading into extreme heat and cold events. These warnings have become a regular seasonal feature of U.S. grid operation rather than a rare occurrence.
  • Extreme weather exposure. Winter Storm Uri in Texas (2021) and subsequent cold-weather events have exposed how vulnerable grids can be when extreme weather simultaneously spikes demand and knocks out generation (including gas plants whose fuel supply or equipment isn't winterized), a combination that traditional planning models sometimes underweight. These events have pushed weatherization and resource adequacy standards up the policy agenda.
  • Slowed coal retirements. Reliability concerns have slowed some planned coal plant retirements and kept gas peaker plants in the resource mix longer than climate targets originally assumed, as grid operators and regulators prioritize near-term reliability over the original decarbonization timeline. This tension between reliability and climate goals is likely to persist as long as firm replacement capacity lags retirements.
  • Capacity market reforms. Regional capacity markets, designed to ensure enough resources are available to meet future peak demand, have been undergoing reforms to better value the specific attributes (like multi-day duration or weather resilience) that matter most for reliability, rather than just raw megawatts of nameplate capacity. These reform debates are contentious because they directly affect which technologies get paid and how much.
