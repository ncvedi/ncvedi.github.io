Nuclear

The renewed interest in nuclear power, including small modular reactors, as a firm, low-carbon source capable of meeting growing electricity demand.

  • Large reactor cost overruns. Large reactor projects in the West—Georgia's Vogtle expansion and the UK's Hinkley Point C—have become cautionary tales on cost and schedule overruns, running years late and billions over budget. These experiences are a big part of why the industry has pivoted its hopes toward small modular reactors that promise standardized, factory-built designs rather than bespoke, site-built megaprojects.
  • SMRs: promise versus delivery. Small modular reactors promise lower per-unit capital costs through factory standardization and faster construction, but none of the SMR developers have yet delivered a commercial plant at the cost or timeline they originally promised, and several high-profile projects have been cancelled or delayed. The technology is still working through the gap between design promises and first-of-a-kind construction reality that has plagued nuclear projects historically.
  • Restarts and corporate power deals. Tech companies signing direct power purchase agreements with nuclear operators—including deals to restart previously retired plants like Three Mile Island—have become a new and significant source of demand and financing for the nuclear industry. This corporate demand is partly driven by the desire for firm, carbon-free power to match data center growth without waiting on grid interconnection queues.
  • Uranium supply chain. Nuclear fuel supply chains are geographically concentrated, with a meaningful share of global uranium enrichment historically tied to Russia, which has created supply security concerns following the war in Ukraine and prompted Western efforts to build out alternative enrichment capacity. This is a slower-moving but real constraint on how fast new nuclear capacity can scale even if reactor construction itself sped up.
  • Government support mechanisms. Loan guarantees, production tax credits, and other government support mechanisms have become increasingly central to nuclear project financing, given how difficult it is to secure conventional project debt for technology with the industry's cost-overrun track record. The gap between nuclear's climate and reliability appeal and its financing difficulty is what most current policy support is trying to bridge.
