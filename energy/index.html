Energy

Notes on the broader energy landscape: how power is generated, stored, and delivered, and how the transition toward lower-carbon sources is playing out against the need for reliable, affordable supply.

  • Grid infrastructure. Why transmission capacity, not generation, is increasingly the binding constraint on new power projects. Interconnection queues in the U.S. now run several years long, with more gigawatts of proposed generation and storage waiting for a grid connection than the existing fleet's total capacity. Building new high-voltage transmission lines faces the same permitting and siting problems as pipelines, so a lot of near-term capacity growth is coming from upgrading existing corridors and pursuing "grid-enhancing technologies" rather than new lines.
  • Renewables. Solar and wind cost curves, intermittency, and the storage buildout needed to make them dispatchable. Solar module costs have fallen so far that in many markets the constraint is no longer generation cost but interconnection, land, and permitting; batteries are following a similar cost curve and are increasingly being paired with solar to shift output into evening peak hours. Wind has had a harder few years, with offshore projects in particular facing cost overruns, cancellations, and financing troubles as interest rates rose and supply chains stayed tight.
  • Nuclear. The renewed interest in nuclear power, including small modular reactors, as a firm low-carbon source. Large reactor projects in the West (Vogtle, Hinkley Point C) have been cautionary tales on cost and schedule overruns, which is a big part of why the industry has pivoted toward SMRs that promise standardized, factory-built designs; none of the SMR players have yet delivered a commercial plant at the cost or timeline they originally promised. Tech companies signing direct power purchase deals with nuclear operators (including restarts of retired plants) is a new and significant source of demand and financing.
  • Baseload and reliability. How grid operators are managing the trade-off between decarbonization and reliability during the transition. As coal and some gas plants retire faster than firm, dispatchable replacements come online, reserve margins have tightened in several U.S. regions, and grid operators have issued increasingly frequent reliability warnings, especially around extreme heat and cold events. This has slowed some coal retirements and kept gas peakers in the resource mix longer than climate targets originally assumed.
  • Demand growth. Data centers, electrification, and industrial demand pushing electricity consumption up after two decades of flat growth. AI training and inference workloads are the most visible driver, with hyperscalers signing power deals directly with generators and even considering building their own generation to skip interconnection queues entirely. EV adoption and the broader push to electrify heating and industrial processes add a second, slower-moving but larger source of structural demand growth on top of that.
