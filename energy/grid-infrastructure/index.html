Grid Infrastructure

Why transmission capacity, not generation, is increasingly the binding constraint on new power projects getting built and connected.

  • Interconnection queues. Interconnection queues in the U.S. now run several years long in many regions, with more gigawatts of proposed generation and storage waiting for a grid connection than the existing fleet's total capacity. Much of what sits in these queues will never actually get built, but the sheer volume of speculative interconnection requests has overwhelmed grid operators' ability to study and approve projects quickly.
  • Transmission permitting and siting. Building new high-voltage transmission lines faces many of the same permitting and siting problems as pipelines—multiple jurisdictions, local opposition, and lengthy environmental review—which is part of why big new interstate transmission projects are rare and slow. Unlike pipelines, transmission lines also often need consensus among neighboring utilities and regional grid operators, adding another layer of coordination difficulty.
  • Grid-enhancing technologies. A lot of near-term capacity growth is coming from upgrading existing corridors and deploying "grid-enhancing technologies"—dynamic line rating, advanced power flow control, reconductoring with higher-capacity cables—rather than building new transmission lines from scratch. These technologies are generally cheaper and faster to deploy, though they have limits compared with genuinely new capacity.
  • Regional grid operator coordination. The U.S. grid is divided among multiple regional transmission organizations and independent system operators, each with their own interconnection processes and planning timelines, which fragments efforts to build transmission that crosses multiple regions. Projects that would connect regions with complementary generation profiles (e.g., wind-heavy Midwest with demand centers) often struggle precisely because they fall between these planning boundaries.
  • FERC policy. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission has pushed reforms aimed at speeding up interconnection studies and encouraging more proactive, forward-looking transmission planning rather than the traditional project-by-project approach, but implementation varies significantly by region and these reforms take years to show up as built capacity.
