Book Reviews

Notes on books I've read, mostly biography and history, on the people and ideas that shaped how I think about the world.

  • Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman! Feynman's memoir, told as a string of anecdotes rather than a straight narrative, from cracking safes at Los Alamos to giving nude portrait lessons to bongo drumming to decoding Mayan glyphs. What comes through is less any specific piece of physics than a whole attitude toward the world: an insistence on figuring things out from first principles, a distrust of authority and credentialism, and a refusal to pretend to understand something he didn't. The book is funny and self-mythologizing in a way that's part of its charm, but the underlying method — play first, formalize later — is the real takeaway.
  • Team of Rivals. Goodwin's account of how Lincoln, a one-term congressman with little executive experience, beat three far more credentialed men (Seward, Chase, Bates) to the 1860 Republican nomination and then put all three in his cabinet. The political genius wasn't just magnanimity for its own sake; Lincoln understood that surrounding himself with strong, ambitious rivals who disagreed with him and each other was better for the country than surrounding himself with loyalists, even though it meant constantly managing egos, leaks, and outright plots against him (Chase ran against him for re-election while serving as Treasury Secretary). The book is as much a study in emotional self-control and patience under pressure as it is a Civil War history.
  • JFK: Coming of Age in the American Century, 1917–1956. Logevall's first volume covers Kennedy from birth through the eve of his run for the vice-presidential nomination, and spends more time than most JFK biographies on the family context: Joseph Kennedy Sr.'s ambassadorship to Britain and his isolationist, appeasement-sympathetic instincts, which JFK's own worldview was partly formed in reaction against. The PT-109 episode and Kennedy's Harvard thesis (published as Why England Slept) both show up as the moments where a somewhat sickly, underachieving younger son started being taken seriously, first by his father and then by the public. It's a useful corrective to the mythologized Camelot version, showing how much of JFK's early trajectory was manufactured, deliberately, by his father's money and connections.
  • The Courtier and the Heretic. Matthew Stewart's dual biography of Leibniz and Spinoza, built around their one documented meeting in 1676, when the ambitious, courtly Leibniz sought out the excommunicated, reclusive Spinoza in The Hague. Stewart frames it as a proxy fight over the future of modern philosophy: Spinoza's God-is-nature pantheism and denial of free will versus Leibniz's lifelong, increasingly strained effort to reconcile the new science with a personal God and moral responsibility, in part because Leibniz needed the patronage of princes who wouldn't tolerate a heretic's conclusions. The book's real argument is that Leibniz privately absorbed more of Spinoza's radicalism than he ever admitted, and spent the rest of his career trying to out-argue a position he could never fully escape.
  • The Red and the Black. Stendhal's novel follows Julien Sorel, a carpenter's son with a photographic memory for Latin scripture, as he schemes his way up through Restoration-era French society by way of the seminary and the households of a provincial mayor and a Parisian marquis. The title's two colors are usually read as the army and the church, the two ladders out of poverty available to an ambitious young man of low birth in post-Napoleonic France, and Julien's constant, exhausting self-monitoring — calculating every gesture and expression for effect — is what makes him one of the first genuinely modern characters in fiction. It's as much a study of hypocrisy and status anxiety in a rigidly stratified society as it is a love story, and the ending refuses to give Julien, or the reader, the redemption the plot seems to be building toward.
  • Crime and Punishment. Dostoevsky's novel of Raskolnikov, an impoverished former student in St. Petersburg who murders a pawnbroker to test a theory that certain extraordinary people are entitled to transgress ordinary morality if the transgression serves a greater purpose. The murder itself happens early; the bulk of the book is the psychological unraveling that follows, as Raskolnikov's rationalization collapses under guilt, paranoia, and the patient, almost pastoral pressure applied by the investigator Porfiry and the prostitute Sonya, who represent two very different paths back to conscience. It's frequently read as a rebuttal to the utilitarian and nihilist ideas circulating in 1860s Russia, arguing that no theory can fully suppress the moral instinct once a person acts on it.
  • The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy. Adams's novel follows Arthur Dent, whose house and then planet are demolished on the same morning, as he's swept off Earth by his friend Ford Prefect, a researcher for a wildly unreliable interstellar travel guide, moments before Earth's demolition to make way for a hyperspace bypass. What follows is less a plot than a string of set pieces — the Total Perspective Vortex, the computer Deep Thought's answer of 42, the Babel fish — each one a joke about bureaucracy, meaninglessness, or the universe's total indifference to human concerns, delivered with a straight face. The comedy is the argument: Adams uses absurdity to make a genuinely bleak premise (nothing about existence is designed to make sense, and no one in charge is competent) go down easy.
